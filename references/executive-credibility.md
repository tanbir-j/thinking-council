# Executive Credibility

Capable advisers often lose ground at one point: conveying expertise to senior executives and bringing them onside. Most of that work happens on the page before any meeting, and it is diagnosable. Run this check on every artefact a CxO, board, owner, or client decision-maker will read or hear. The CFO in the room seat leads it.

## The 30-second read

Simulate the executive reading the first paragraph, the first slide, or the subject line and opening sentence, on a phone, between meetings. Report three things in the executive's own terms:

1. What they now believe the author thinks, and whether they can tell.
2. What they now believe about the author: a supplier, a competent adviser, a peer, or someone they would call before the board meeting.
3. Whether they keep reading, and if not, where they stopped.

If the executive cannot answer question one after 30 seconds, nothing else in the artefact matters yet.

## Expertise signals audit

Experts leave markers that generalists cannot fake. Check for presence and quality; list what is present, what is missing, and what would be cheapest to add.

Signals present in expert work:

- **The trap named before the client hits it.** A specific failure the author has seen, stated before it happens here. "In carve-outs the TSA exit date drives every other date, and it is usually set by someone who has never run a close."
- **The counterintuitive call, made calmly.** A recommendation the executive did not expect, stated without hedging and with the reason attached.
- **Pattern from experience, with texture.** "I have seen this three times" is a claim; the third time, with what went wrong, is a signal. The author must supply these; the skill marks the slot and never fabricates one.
- **Numbers with provenance and range.** "Close in 5 to 7 days, based on the last two programmes at this scale" beats "significant acceleration."
- **Fluency in the executive's constraints.** Board cycle, audit committee calendar, covenant tests, budget round, close calendar, the lender's reporting pack, the auditor's year-end timetable. An adviser who knows when the executive's next bad week is has done this before.
- **Judgement with a stake in it.** "I would do X. The risk is Y, and here is how I would watch for it." The author's exposure is visible.
- **Calm brevity.** The confidence to say less. Length signals uncertainty or billing.
- **Knowing what not to do.** Naming the fashionable move and explaining why not here.

Anti-signals that read as inexperience or salesmanship:

- frameworks before findings; the model arrives before the observation
- hedging clusters: "may", "could", "potentially", "it depends" without saying on what
- activity lists instead of outcomes: what was done rather than what changed
- explaining basics the executive already knows
- length, appendices as proof of effort, and decks that argue by volume
- borrowed authority: "best practice suggests", "leading organisations", survey statistics doing the work the author's judgement should do
- the technology leading the argument
- describing the journey without naming the destination and the first step
- overclaiming certainty on things nobody can know, and underclaiming on things the author plainly does know

## Register check: peer, not supplicant

The register is the fastest tell. Check the artefact against paired constructions and report which side it sits on.

| Supplicant register | Peer register |
|---|---|
| "You may wish to consider" | "I would do X" |
| "Best practice suggests" | "I have seen this fail when" |
| "We would be delighted to" | "Here is what I would do first" |
| "There are a number of options" | "There are two real options and I would take the second" |
| "Subject to further analysis" | "I am confident on X and not yet on Y" |
| "Leveraging our methodology" | "The way I would run this is" |
| Ending with an offer of help | Ending with a decision required |

The peer register is not arrogance. It is judgement stated with its risks attached and the decision left with the executive.

## Commercial fluency

Every recommendation must translate into the executive's own metrics before it counts. Check that the artefact speaks in cash, EBITDA, working capital, close days, forecast accuracy, headcount and cost to serve, audit findings, covenant headroom, valuation, or whatever this executive is measured on. A recommendation with no line to one of these is a finding: either connect it or cut it.

Test the "why now": what in this executive's calendar, ownership, or results makes this the moment. If the artefact does not know, it does not know the executive.

## Alliance mechanics

Being right is necessary and insufficient. Use the trust equation from Maister, Green, and Galford (The Trusted Advisor, 2000) as a cited diagnostic, not as original material: trust rises with credibility, reliability, and intimacy, and falls with the adviser's visible self-orientation.

Run the personal stakes map for each named executive:

- What are they exposed on if this goes wrong, and does the artefact protect them?
- What do they need to be able to say to their board, owner, or CEO, and does the artefact hand them the sentence?
- What do they fear, and has the artefact named it without amplifying it?
- Whose agenda does this serve, and can the executive see themselves as the author of the decision?
- Does the ask transfer a decision or transfer work? Executives accept decisions and resent work.

Techniques that build alliance on the page:

- Options framing that leaves the decision, not the work: two or three real options, a recommendation, and the risk of each, in under a page.
- Making the recommendation their idea: anchor it to something they have already said or done.
- Naming the obstacle they will face internally, and giving them the counter.
- Small, early, visible wins sequenced before the structural work, so they have something to report.
- Protecting them from surprise: no finding reaches a board that the executive has not seen first.

Self-orientation tells to remove: selling the next phase inside the current deliverable, credentials paragraphs, the author's methodology as a protagonist, gratitude and enthusiasm as filler.

## In the room

For talking points, meeting prep, or interview preparation, add:

- the first two minutes: what the author says before anyone asks a question, and what it establishes
- the question the author wants the executive to ask, and how the opening sets it up
- the answer to "what would you do if you were me", prepared in advance, one sentence plus one risk
- the answer to "why should I believe you", prepared as one specific pattern from experience, not a credential
- the one number the author will be asked for, and its provenance
- what the author will refuse to claim, said out loud, because declining to overclaim is itself a signal

## Failure patterns specific to finance transformation advisers

- Leading with the target operating model diagram; the executive wants the problem first.
- Leading with technology; the executive has been sold technology before.
- Treating the CFO as the only audience when the CEO, chair, or owner is the real decision-maker.
- Presenting the diagnostic without the first step, or the roadmap without the diagnostic.
- Underestimating the political cost of the finding to the person who commissioned it.
- Writing for the finance team when the reader is the board.
- Sounding like the last three firms that pitched.

## Credibility verdict

Give one of four levels, with evidence and the single change that would raise it one level:

- **Supplier.** Competent, generic, replaceable. The executive would take the work and not remember the author.
- **Competent adviser.** Sound and clear. The executive would use it and might call again.
- **Peer.** The executive reads judgement with stakes attached, in their own metrics, and adjusts their thinking.
- **Trusted adviser.** The executive would call the author before the board meeting, and would say so to their chair.

Never present this verdict as an average of the others. It is a separate question with a separate answer.
