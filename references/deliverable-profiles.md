# Deliverable Profiles

Identify the deliverable before reviewing or drafting. If the user has not stated it, infer it from purpose, audience, and form, state the inference, and say which findings would change if the inference is wrong. Published work (articles, white papers, social posts, carousels) uses [article-profile.md](article-profile.md) and [venue-profiles.md](venue-profiles.md) instead.

Each profile gives the reader's behaviour, what good looks like, the gates that apply, the failure patterns, and the form the Drafting Room produces.

## Proposal or statement of work

- Reader: a buyer comparing the author against two or three alternatives, reading the summary and the commercials first, the approach second, the team last.
- Good: the author's understanding of the client's situation is more precise than the client's own brief; the approach is a sequence of decisions rather than activities; the commercials are simple; the risk section names what could go wrong and what the author would do about it.
- Gates: Strategy partner; CFO in the room as the buyer; executive credibility check. Sun Tzu on whether to bid at all and on which ground.
- Failure patterns: methodology as protagonist, the same approach for every client, activities without decisions, credentials before understanding, the ask buried in the commercials.
- Form: full draft permitted. Slots for engagement-specific experience.

## Diagnostic findings or current-state assessment

- Reader: the sponsor who commissioned it and is exposed by it, then the executives it implicates.
- Good: findings ranked by materiality; each finding evidenced at the strength asserted; root cause separated from symptom; the political cost of each finding to its owner anticipated; the first step named.
- Gates: Audit partner leads; Sherlock; CFO in the room as the sponsor. No finding reaches a wider audience that the sponsor has not seen first.
- Failure patterns: materiality inversion, findings that are opinions, symptom lists, a diagnostic that ends without a first step, blame allocated to people who will read it.
- Form: full draft permitted. Evidence labelled by provenance: observed, reported, inferred.

## Board or ExCo paper, steering committee paper

- Reader: the chair or CFO reading the cover page and the decision required, then a board member reading only for what could embarrass them.
- Good: decision required stated on page one in one sentence; context in one paragraph; two or three real options with the recommendation and its risk; what happens if the board does nothing; the ask is a decision, not work. One to three pages.
- Gates: Strategy partner; Audit partner where numbers or controls appear; CFO in the room as the chair; wild card audit committee chair for governance papers.
- Failure patterns: the decision required arriving on page four, options that are one option and two straw men, no do-nothing case, papers that report activity, surprises for the sponsor.
- Form: full draft permitted. Skeleton in [drafting-room.md](drafting-room.md).

## Business case

- Reader: the CFO, then finance, then the audit committee if the number is large.
- Good: baseline stated and sourced; benefits with owners, dates, and the mechanism that produces them; costs including internal time; the sensitivity that would kill the case; the option value of waiting; the case for doing nothing costed honestly.
- Gates: Audit partner leads; PE operating partner where the owner is PE; Strategy partner on the top-down read.
- Failure patterns: benefits without baseline, savings that are cost avoidance in disguise, benefits realised in year one of a three-year programme, no downside case, internal cost ignored, technology cost without change cost.
- Form: full draft permitted with every number flagged by provenance. Never invent a number; mark the slot.

## Operating model or target state design

- Reader: the CFO for the destination, the finance leadership team for what it does to them, the CEO or CHRO for headcount and structure.
- Good: the destination described by what decisions get made where and by whom, not by boxes; the transition sequenced; the people consequences stated plainly; what the design deliberately does not do.
- Gates: Thrawn on whether it solves the right problem; Ted Lasso on adoption; wild card Head of FP&A for what breaks on Monday; CFO in the room.
- Failure patterns: the diagram before the decision rights, a target state with no transition, headcount consequences left implicit, service catalogues nobody will read, a design that describes the author's last client.
- Form: scaffold or draft depending on the artefact. Figures get a dedicated review.

## Transformation roadmap or programme plan

- Reader: the sponsor for what they can report, the programme team for sequence, the owner for dates and value.
- Good: sequenced by dependency and value, not by workstream; early visible wins before structural work; decision points named; what is deliberately deferred and why; resourcing honest about management bandwidth.
- Gates: PE operating partner on value and speed; Sun Tzu on sequence; Ted Lasso on capacity; Audit partner where benefits appear.
- Failure patterns: Gantt as argument, everything starting in month one, no decision points, benefits at the end, roadmaps that ignore the close calendar and year-end.
- Form: full draft permitted.

## Executive email, one-pager, or talking points

- Reader: the executive, on a phone, in under a minute.
- Good: the answer in the first sentence; the reason in the second; the ask as a decision with a date; nothing else. Under 150 words for an email. Talking points follow the in-the-room section of [executive-credibility.md](executive-credibility.md).
- Gates: CFO in the room; executive credibility check; register check is mandatory.
- Failure patterns: context before answer, the ask at the end, offers of help, more than one ask, the supplicant register, anything that requires scrolling.
- Form: full draft. Two variants at most.

## Workshop design or interview guide

- Reader: the facilitator (usually the author) and the participants who will judge whether their time was respected.
- Good: each session produces a decision or an artefact; questions sequenced from concrete to abstract; the hardest question placed where trust has been built; a plan for the participant who dominates and the one who disengages; the output format agreed before the session.
- Gates: Ted Lasso leads; Mary Poppins on sequencing; Socrates on question quality; for interviews, Sherlock on what evidence each question is designed to surface.
- Failure patterns: sessions that produce discussion, questions that lead the witness, agendas that fill time, no output artefact, the same guide for every interviewee.
- Form: full draft permitted.

## Client deck

- Reader: the executive reading the executive summary and the action titles only, then whoever is asked to check the numbers.
- Good: the storyline reads as an argument from the titles alone; one idea per slide; every exhibit has a so-what in its title; the executive summary stands alone; the ask is on a slide of its own.
- Gates: Strategy partner leads; CFO in the room on the 30-second read; Audit partner on any number; Mary Poppins on clutter.
- Failure patterns: descriptive titles, exhibits without a point, slides that argue by volume, the answer on slide twelve, appendices treated as evidence, a deck written for the finance team and shown to the board.
- Form: storyline first as a numbered list of action titles; slides only after the storyline is agreed.

## Consulting report

- Reader: the sponsor reading the executive summary and the recommendations, then the people named in the findings reading for what it says about them, then whoever must implement it.
- Good: the executive summary stands alone and states the answer; each recommendation carries the reason it produces the benefit, its prerequisite, its owner, and how the client will know it worked; findings are separated from interpretation; the report says what was out of scope and what it could not establish.
- Gates: Strategy partner on the top-down read; Audit partner on evidence; CFO in the room as the sponsor; the meaning gate on the recommendations, where missing links cost the most.
- Failure patterns: a summary that summarises the method, recommendations as a list of activities, findings and opinions mixed, length offered as proof of effort, the same recommendation the last adviser made with no reason it will land this time.
- Form: full draft permitted. Recommendations drafted as claim, reason, prerequisite, owner, test.

## Financial or analytical model

- Reader: the decision-maker who sees only the outputs, then the reviewer who must trust the workings, then whoever maintains it next year.
- Good: the decision the model serves is stated on the front sheet; inputs, calculations, and outputs are separated; every assumption has a source, a date, and an owner; one formula per row; check totals reconcile to a known figure; the two or three inputs that move the answer are identified and flexed; a stranger can trace any output to its inputs.
- Gates: Audit partner leads; the model auditor wild card traces one output end to end; PE operating partner where the model supports a valuation or a lender case; Sherlock on what the model assumes without saying. Apply the model checks in [close-read.md](close-read.md) section 15 to one block of logic at a time.
- Failure patterns: hard-coded numbers inside formulas, plugs and overrides, a precise answer built on a guessed driver, no sensitivity, outputs that cannot be traced, a model that answers a different question from the one the decision needs, complexity that hides the two assumptions that matter.
- Form: review and report. Never change a number in the author's model; list each finding with its cell or range, its effect on the answer where that can be estimated, and the fix. Building a model is outside this skill's Drafting Room; scaffold the structure and the assumption register instead.

## Thinking-partner brief (Open Field)

- Reader: the author.
- Good: the problem restated more precisely than the author put it; hypotheses ranked; the angle the author has not considered; the ten-out-of-ten version of the eventual deliverable; the one thing to find out next.
- Gates: none. This is phase one as output.
- Failure patterns: answering the question as asked when a better question exists, lists of considerations without a view, council vocabulary leaking into what should be free thinking.
- Form: prose. Ends with the ranked angles and the next move.

## Consultancy scorecard

Score each relevant dimension from 1 to 5 using the scale and calibration anchors in [article-profile.md](article-profile.md). Never average. Omit dimensions the deliverable does not carry and say so.

1. Problem definition: is it the right problem, stated more precisely than the client stated it?
2. Commercial relevance: does every recommendation connect to a metric the executive is measured on?
3. Evidence: are claims supported at the strength asserted, with provenance?
4. Judgement: is there a recommendation with a stake in it, or a menu?
5. Executive readability: does the 30-second read deliver the answer and the ask?
6. Feasibility and adoption: can this organisation, with this management bandwidth, do it?
7. Risk awareness: are the ways this goes wrong named, with the author's response?
8. Credibility signals: does the artefact carry the markers of someone who has done this before?

A 4 on judgement with a 2 on evidence is a finding, not an average of 3.
