# Drafting Room

The council as a drafting room. Use when the user asks for a deliverable to be produced rather than reviewed. Open Field runs first; the storyline is agreed second; the artefact is produced third; a Council Glance on the artefact runs last.

## Form decision

| Deliverable | Form | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Article, white paper, series part | Bullet scaffold for the author to write | Published prose faces an AI-detection gate. Drafts written by the author from scaffolds are the only ones that pass reliably. |
| Personal blog piece | Draft on explicit request | The author's own channel; still style-guide compliant and self-swept. |
| Social post or carousel copy | Draft on explicit request | Short form; not scanned; the author edits. |
| Proposal, board paper, business case, diagnostic, roadmap, deck, email, talking points, workshop design | Full draft | Client work; the author edits and owns. |

When unsure, ask once. Never produce finished published prose unasked.

## Scaffold standard (published work)

A scaffold is a complete argument the author can write from without re-planning. It contains:

- the governing thought in one sentence and the desired reader response
- section headings with a word budget each, summing to the venue budget
- for each section: the claim, the supporting moves in order, and the evidence placed where it should land mid-sentence, with source
- anecdote and experience slots marked for the author, with the job each slot must do ("the trap you saw on a real engagement; one sentence; lands the cost of delay")
- transitions stated as the question each section leaves for the next
- callback inventory, each usable once
- craft notes on jokes, register, and what to cut if the piece runs long

Scaffolds carry no finished sentences other than the governing thought and any line that must survive verbatim across a series.

## Draft standard (client work)

- Storyline before artefact. Write the governing thought, the key line, and the support as a numbered list first. Ask for agreement on the storyline when the deliverable is a deck, board paper, or proposal; proceed directly for emails and talking points.
- Answer first. The first sentence of any executive-facing draft states the answer or the decision required.
- Provenance labels on every factual claim while drafting: FACT (sourced), PATTERN (repeated behaviour or experience the author has confirmed), INFERENCE (the draft's deduction). Strip the labels only when the author confirms the facts.
- Experience slots. Where an expertise signal belongs, mark the slot: "[Author: one specific from a real engagement; the trap, what went wrong, one sentence]". Never fabricate client facts, numbers, or experience. A draft with invented specifics is worse than a scaffold.
- Register: peer, per [executive-credibility.md](executive-credibility.md). Run the register check on the draft.
- The author's style guide applies in full: find it (the author profile names it) before drafting. The profile also sets the language variant for each venue. With no guide available, write plainly, keep evidence inside the sentence it supports, and avoid the fallback patterns listed in the self-sweep below.
- Length: the profile's budget is a wall. Executive email under 150 words. Board paper one to three pages. Deck storyline before any slide.

## Self-sweep before handoff

1. Reasoning check. Run the Close Read reasoning pass ([close-read.md](close-read.md) section 3) on the draft before any style work: each section has a distinct job, each claim its link and consequence, and anything you supplied that the author did not is marked PROPOSED for confirmation.
2. AI-signature sweep against the style guide's pattern list (fallback: em dashes; "X is not Y, it is Z" negation; same-scaffold parallel sentences; "The [noun]" opener after a short sentence; manufactured enthusiasm and hype intensifiers; descriptive triads). Fix what is found; report what was fixed.
3. Register check: no supplicant constructions in executive-facing drafts.
4. Council Glance on the draft: verdict, three findings, next move. State plainly what the draft would fail if it went out as is. A draft handed over without its own Glance is incomplete.

## Skeletons

Use these as defaults, not templates to fill. Cut what the situation does not need.

### Board, ExCo, or steering committee paper

1. Decision required (one sentence, page one, with the date it is needed by)
2. Why now (one paragraph: what changed)
3. Options (two or three, each with what it costs, what it gets, and its main risk; the do-nothing case costed honestly)
4. Recommendation and the risk of it
5. What the board will see next, and when
6. Appendix: evidence, if the paper needs one at all

### Executive email

1. The answer or the decision required
2. The reason, in one or two sentences, in the executive's metric
3. The ask, with a date
4. The next step if they agree, so agreement costs them nothing

Under 150 words. No greeting longer than a name. No closing offer of help.

### Proposal

1. Your situation as we understand it (more precise than the brief; the trap named)
2. What we would do, as a sequence of decisions you will make, with what each one unlocks
3. What you will have at the end, and by when
4. Who does the work and why them, in one paragraph
5. What could go wrong and what we would do about it
6. Commercials, simply
7. The decision you need to make and by when

### Business case

1. The decision and the number, on one line
2. Baseline: where we are, sourced
3. Benefits: mechanism, owner, date, and the evidence for the mechanism
4. Costs: external, internal time, change, and the cost of the transition period
5. Sensitivities: the assumption that kills the case, and how likely it is
6. The do-nothing case and the option value of waiting
7. Recommendation, with the risk stated

### Client deck storyline

Write the action titles as a numbered list that reads as the whole argument on its own. Each title is a full sentence with a so-what. The executive summary is title one. The ask is the final title. Only when the list argues correctly are slides built beneath it.

### Diagnostic findings

1. The three findings that matter, ranked by materiality, on page one
2. For each: the evidence (observed, reported, inferred), the root cause, the consequence in the executive's metric, and the first step
3. What was not examined and why it does not change the conclusion, or does
4. What the sponsor should say to their board about this
5. Detailed findings in an appendix, ranked the same way

### Talking points

1. First two minutes, verbatim: what is established before any question
2. The question the author wants asked, and how the opening plants it
3. Prepared answers: "what would you do if you were me", "why should I believe you", the one number and its provenance
4. What the author will decline to claim, and the sentence that declines it
5. The close: the decision requested and the next step

## After drafting

Report in this order: the storyline as agreed, the artefact, the self-sweep results, the Glance. Then stop. Do not append options, offers, or alternative versions unless the profile calls for two variants.
